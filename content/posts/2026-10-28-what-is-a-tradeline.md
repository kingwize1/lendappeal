> **Short answer:** A tradeline is any credit account listed on your credit report, such as a credit card, auto loan, mortgage or student loan. Each one records the lender, account type, open date, limit or loan amount, balance and month-by-month payment history. When people talk about "buying a tradeline," they usually mean being added as an authorized user to an older, well-managed credit card so that account's history can report on their file.

## What a tradeline contains

Every account on a credit report is a tradeline. The credit bureaus (Experian, Equifax and TransUnion) store the following for each one:

- **Creditor name** and account type (revolving, installment, mortgage or open)
- **Date opened**, which sets the account's age
- **Credit limit** (cards) or original loan amount (installment loans)
- **Current balance** and the balance reported at the last statement
- **Payment history**, shown as a grid of on-time and late months
- **Status**, such as open, closed, paid or in collections
- **Your role** on the account: individual, joint or authorized user

## Why tradelines matter for your score

FICO publishes the broad weights it uses for its scores: payment history about 35%, amounts owed (including utilization) about 30%, length of credit history about 15%, new credit about 10% and credit mix about 10%. A tradeline can touch several of those at once: an older account raises your average age, a high limit with a low balance lowers your utilization, and a long run of on-time months adds positive history.

## The two kinds of tradeline you can hold

| | Primary tradeline | Authorized-user tradeline |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the account | You | Someone else |
| Who is legally responsible for the debt | You | The primary cardholder |
| Do you get a card | Yes | Often no |
| Builds an independent credit history | Yes | Only borrowed history |
| Can it be removed if the owner misses payments | No, you own it | You can ask to be removed |

For a deeper comparison, read our guide to [primary vs authorized-user tradelines](/blog/primary-vs-authorized-user-tradelines/).

## How an authorized-user tradeline works

1. The primary cardholder asks the issuer to add you as an authorized user, usually with your name, date of birth and Social Security number.
2. After the next statement closes, the issuer may report the account to the credit bureaus under your name.
3. The account appears on your report with its own age, limit, balance and payment history.
4. When the authorized-user period ends, you are removed and the account typically disappears from your report.

Two details people overlook. First, issuers are not required to report authorized users to the bureaus, and they decide which bureaus receive the data, so the same account may show on one report and not another. Second, myFICO notes that authorized-user accounts can help or hurt: the primary holder's missed payments or high balances can show on your file too. See [when tradelines post](/blog/when-do-tradelines-post-statement-dates/) for the timing details.

## What a tradeline cannot do

- It cannot guarantee a score increase, an approval or funding. Results depend on your whole credit file.
- It cannot replace accounts you own. Newer FICO versions weight authorized-user accounts less than your own accounts, according to myFICO.
- It does not erase negative items such as collections or late payments.
- Some underwriters discount it. Fannie Mae's manual underwriting rules generally exclude authorized-user tradelines unless specific conditions are met. More in [do lenders count authorized-user tradelines](/blog/do-lenders-count-authorized-user-tradelines/).

## How to pick a tradeline

1. **Check the statement date.** The account reports after it closes, so the date decides which cycle you land in.
2. **Look at age and limit.** Older accounts and higher limits have more to offer a thin file.
3. **Confirm low utilization.** A $500 balance on a $10,000 limit is 5%; $9,000 is 90%. The first helps, the second does not.
4. **Check for a warranty** and read what counts as a covered non-posting.
5. **Remove blockers first**, such as an active fraud alert or credit freeze, which can stop an account from posting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a tradeline the same as a credit card?
No. A credit card is an account. The tradeline is the record of that account on a credit report. One card creates one tradeline for each person who is reported on it.

### Do authorized-user tradelines show on all three bureaus?
Not always. Experian notes that issuers may choose what to report and to how many bureaus. Check all three reports after the account posts.

### How many tradelines is enough?
There is no magic number. Scoring models look at the mix, ages and balances together. Many lenders like to see several open accounts with on-time history.

### Is buying a tradeline legal?
Being added as an authorized user is a standard credit card feature. How you use the resulting credit file matters: never misstate your accounts or income on a lender's application. This article is educational and is not legal advice.
